# Amazon: the online bookstore that lost 93% in the crash and became one of the most valuable companies in the world

Span: 1997-05 to today.

## The story

Jeff Bezos left a Wall Street hedge fund in 1994, founded the company in his garage in Bellevue, Washington, and opened Amazon.com as "Earth's Biggest Bookstore" in July 1995. When it listed on May 15, 1997 (3 million shares at $18), it had 1996 sales of $15.7 million and a net loss of $5.8 million, and it said openly that it would keep losing money to grow. A player in 1997 is buying the most famous shop on the young World Wide Web, with sales multiplying every quarter ($2.2 million in the second quarter of 1996, $27.9 million a year later) and no profit in sight.

The bubble years were spectacular. Amazon added music (June 1998), video, the UK and Germany, toys and electronics, bought start-ups with its own shares and invested in others, Pets.com among them. Sales went from $148 million (1997) to $610 million (1998), $1.64 billion (1999) and $2.76 billion (2000). The shares were split three times in fifteen months, rose 126% in June 1998 alone, and gained 1,108% in the twelve months to March 1999; in December 1998 an analyst's "$400 within a year" call was reached in weeks.

Then investors stopped funding losses. From the March 1999 high to September 2001 the shares fell 93%. Amazon lost $720 million in 1999 and $1.4 billion in 2000, wrote off its stakes in other dot-coms, and in January 2001 cut 1,300 jobs and closed a distribution centre. It survived because it had raised money while it could and kept growing sales; in the fourth quarter of 2001 it made its first net profit, $5 million on over $1 billion of sales, and the doubters began to retreat.

The middle years were a slow, uneven climb punctuated by violent earnings reactions: +36% in July 2005, -30% in July 2006 when profits fell under heavy spending, +54% in April 2007 when profits doubled. Behind it Bezos built what made the company: the marketplace for other sellers, Prime, Fulfillment by Amazon (2006), the Kindle reader and Amazon Web Services, whose storage and computing rental began in 2006. When Amazon first reported AWS separately in April 2015 ("a $5 billion business and still growing fast"), investors saw that the cloud was the profit engine, and the shares started a decade-long rise: Whole Foods (2017), the pandemic boom of 2020 (175,000 extra workers, shares about +75% in the year), Bezos handing over to Andy Jassy (2021).

The last stretch brought the first real setback since 2001, a loss in early 2022 and a halving of the share price, followed by 27,000 job cuts, and then a new boom on artificial intelligence: the investment in Anthropic from 2023 and accelerating AWS growth (+28% in early 2026). From the IPO price to 2026 the share rose more than 3,000-fold in game terms, despite losing 93% along the way.

## Best and worst moments

- Best twelve months: +1,108% from 1998-03 to 1999-03; best month 1998-06 (+126%), also 1998-12 (+67%) and 1998-11 (+52%).
- Worst stretch: -93% from 1999-03 to 2001-09; worst months 2001-02 (-41%), 2000-12 (-37%), 2000-11 (-33%), 2001-09 (-33%).
- Turning points: 2002-01 (+31%) first profit; 2007-04 (+54%) profits double; 2015-04 AWS revealed and 2015-07 (+24%); 2022-04 (-24%) the first loss in years; 2026-04 (+27%) AI cloud growth.

## Unexplained moves

- 1997-09 (+86%): no Amazon announcement found for September 1997; checked the 10-Q for the quarter to September (filed November 14, 1997) and the 8-K of November 1997 (the $75 million loan, which came later). Possibly the general enthusiasm for internet shares; not confirmed.
- 2000-08 (+38%): a rebound after the shares fell 17% in July. Wikipedia mentions a ten-year deal under which Toys "R" Us became the exclusive toy supplier on Amazon in 2000, but gives no month; no August 2000 Amazon filing was found to confirm it.
- 2000-12 (-37%): part of the late-2000 collapse of internet shares (the Nasdaq fell sharply in November and December 2000); no Amazon-specific December event found in its filings.
- 2001-02 (-41%): followed the January 30 report with the 1,300 job cuts (covered in 2001-01, when it became public); the market fell 9% in February 2001. No separate February event found.
- 2001-11 (+62%): rebound from the post-September 11 low (the shares had fallen 33% in September); the 10-Q of October 30, 2001 was checked, no November event found. The first profit, reported in January 2002, is covered there.
