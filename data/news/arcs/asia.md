# Asia rising: Japan's bubble and bust, then China's century

Span: 1979-12 to today.

## The story

When the game opens, Japan is the economy everyone wants to copy. Ezra Vogel's *Japan as Number One* (1979) is a best-seller, Japan overtakes America as the biggest car maker in 1980, and Washington answers with "voluntary" export limits in 1981 and chip sanctions in 1987. The decisive event is the Plaza Accord of September 1985: the rich countries agree to push the dollar down, and the yen goes from about 240 per dollar to about 120 by the end of 1987. To fight the strong-yen recession the Bank of Japan cuts its rate in five steps from 5% to a record 2.5% (February 1987) and keeps it there until May 1989. The cheap money flows into land and shares. The Nikkei goes from 13,000 at the end of 1985 to 38,915.87 on 29 December 1989; four of the world's five most valuable companies are Japanese banks, led by the Industrial Bank of Japan, which is in the game. Japanese buyers take Columbia Pictures and Rockefeller Center.

The bust starts the next week. The Bank of Japan, under Yasushi Mieno, raises rates to 6% by August 1990 and the Finance Ministry caps property lending in March 1990. The Nikkei halves by October 1990 and falls to 14,309 in August 1992. The banks sit on bad loans backed by collapsing land prices, the yen soars to under 80 per dollar in April 1995, and in November 1997 the crisis breaks open: Hokkaido Takushoku Bank and Yamaichi Securities fail. In 1998 the Long-Term Credit Bank is nationalised, the yen swings from 147 to 112 per dollar within months, and the state puts 9.3 trillion yen into the big banks. IBJ ends in Mizuho in 2000. Zero rates, quantitative easing (2001-2006), a Nikkei low below 8,000 in 2003 and below 7,000 in October 2008, the earthquake and Fukushima in March 2011: Japan's "lost decades".

The Asian crisis of 1997-98 links the two halves. Thailand floats the baht on 2 July 1997, the day after Hong Kong returns to China; Indonesia and Korea need record IMF rescues, Hong Kong's crash drags down Wall Street in October 1997, and the contagion's last wave, Russia's default in August 1998, gives the S&P 500 its worst month since 1987.

China's story runs the other way. Deng Xiaoping's reforms of December 1978 bring the special economic zones (Shenzhen, 1980), stock exchanges in Shanghai and Shenzhen (1990) and, after the Tiananmen crackdown of June 1989 freezes reform, Deng's southern tour of 1992 and growth of about 13% a year. China joins the WTO on 11 December 2001 and becomes the world's factory; its demand drives the commodity supercycle that carries the miners in the game (Xstrata, Glencore) and the oil service firm Schlumberger. Its 4 trillion yuan stimulus of November 2008 pulls the world's commodity markets out of the crash, and in 2010 China passes Japan as the second-largest economy. From 2015 the story turns: a stock bubble bursts, the August 2015 devaluation and the January 2016 circuit breakers shake Wall Street, the trade war with the US begins in July 2018 and peaks with tariffs of 145% in April 2025, Beijing cracks down on its tech champions, zero-Covid locks down Shanghai, and the property bust that began with Evergrande in 2021 drags on.

Japan meanwhile revives. Abenomics (from December 2012) and Haruhiko Kuroda's bond-buying weaken the yen; Buffett buys the trading houses in 2020; the Nikkei regains its 1989 record on 22 February 2024, 34 years later, and the Bank of Japan ends negative rates in March 2024. After a carry-trade panic on 5 August 2024 (-12.4% in a day), the Nikkei goes on past 50,000 in October 2025 under Sanae Takaichi and past 60,000 and 70,000 in 2026, while the yen falls to its weakest since the 1980s and Japan spends a record 15.4 trillion yen defending it in the summer of 2026.

## Best and worst moments

- Plaza Accord, 1985-09: the yen's doubling begins (no single big move in the game; IBJ joins in May 1985).
- Bubble years: IBJ +45% in 1986-03 (rate cuts), +69% in 1987-01 (Nikkei past 20,000).
- Peak: Nikkei 38,915.87 on 29 December 1989.
- The bust: IBJ -29% in 1990-03 (rate rise to 5.25%, yen at 155-158); worst stretch -92% from 1989-12 to 1998-08.
- Rebounds: IBJ +32% in 1990-10, +40% in 1992-08 (Nikkei low then bounce), +31% in 1998-10 (yen surge, LTCB nationalised), +35% in 1999-03 (public money, zero rates).
- Asian crisis: S&P 500 -3% in 1997-10 (Hong Kong crash), -15% in 1998-08 (Russia's default); IBJ -29% in 1998-08.
- Global crash in Japan: Sony -25% in 2008-10 (Nikkei below 7,000, yen surge).
- Abenomics: Sony +33% in 2013-01 (yen from 87 to 91).
- China scares: S&P 500 -6% in 2015-08 (devaluation), -5% in 2016-01 (circuit breakers).
- Trade war: S&P 500 -9% in 2018-12, -7% in 2019-05; +6% in 2025-05 (Geneva truce).
- Return of Japan: Nikkei record 39,098.68 on 22 February 2024.

## Unexplained moves

These IBJ months are not given a `move` here; the IBJ company arc should explain them.

- 1986-07, IBJ +36%: the yen rose from about 164 to 154 per dollar (Bank of England daily rates), worth about 6%; no Japan-wide event found for the rest.
- 1987-04, IBJ +41%: the dollar fell below 140 yen (about 4% in the month) and the US imposed chip sanctions; neither explains a rise of this size, and no source was found for a bank-share rally that month.
- 1997-01, IBJ -27%: the yen weakened from about 116 to 121 per dollar; reports of bank worries early in 1997 could not be confirmed from an accessible source.
- 1997-06, IBJ +29%: the yen strengthened from about 127 (end of April) to 114 per dollar (end of June); no confirmed Japan-wide event for the rest.

Sony's biggest months outside 2008-10 and 2013-01 are company events, left to the Sony arc. The 1987-10 crash has a beat here (Tokyo and Hong Kong) but no `move`, because its cause lies in New York; the 2020 pandemic months are left to the pandemic arc (this arc has the Wuhan lockdown in 2020-01).
