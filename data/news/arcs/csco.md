# Cisco: from router start-up to the world's most valuable company, an 86% crash, and a quarter-century climb back as a dividend payer

Span: 1990-02 to today.

## The story

Cisco was founded in December 1984 by Leonard Bosack and Sandy Lerner, who ran computer facilities at Stanford and built routers that let different computer networks talk to each other. When it listed on Nasdaq on February 16, 1990 (2.8 million shares at $18, first-day close $22.25, a market value of about $224 million), it was a small, profitable company with about $28 million of sales. Venture capitalist Don Valentine had installed John Morgridge as chief executive in 1988; the founders were pushed out in August 1990 and sold all their shares that December for about $170 million. For a player in 1990 the pitch is simple: companies are wiring up their computers, and Cisco makes the boxes that connect the networks, with sales and profits doubling every year.

The first five years were a near-uninterrupted run. Sales went from $70 million (fiscal 1990) to $183 million (1991), $340 million (1992) and $1.24 billion (1994), with profits growing as fast. From the October 1990 market low the shares more than tripled in twelve months. In 1993 Cisco made its first acquisition (Crescendo, $95 million) and began the strategy that defined it: buying start-ups with its own highly valued shares, about 70 of them between 1993 and 2000, including the switch makers that became the Catalyst line, StrataCom (about $4 billion, 1996) and Cerent and Monterey ($7.4 billion, 1999). John Chambers became chief executive in January 1995 and sold Cisco as the plumbing of the "Internet Economy". The shares were split nine times between 1990 and March 2000. Sales reached $8.5 billion in fiscal 1998 and $12.2 billion in fiscal 1999; the market value passed $100 billion in July 1998 and about $555 billion on March 27, 2000, when Cisco briefly overtook Microsoft as the most valuable company in the world. In game terms a share bought at the IPO for 0.08 was worth 77 at the end of March 2000, nearly a thousandfold.

The collapse was as fast as the rise. Cisco had committed to long supply contracts in 2000 and its forecasting saw no slowdown ("We haven't seen any sign of a slowdown", November 2000) while bookings were still up 70%. On February 6, 2001 it missed analysts' estimates for the first time in years; on March 9 it announced the first layoffs in its history; on April 16 it warned that sales would fall and raised the job cuts to 8,500; on May 8 it reported a $2.69 billion quarterly loss including a $2.2 billion write-off of excess inventory, after bookings swung from +70% to -30% within a few months. The shares fell 37% in February and 33% in March 2001, and from the March 2000 peak to September 2002 they lost 86% (about $430 billion of market value). Cisco never came close to failing: it held over $20 billion in cash, stayed profitable before special charges, and gained market share as rivals shrank. Sales grew again from late 2002.

From 2003 Cisco became a large, slow, cash-rich company. It tried consumer markets (Linksys 2003, Scientific-Atlanta for $6.9 billion in 2005, the Flip camera) and later retreated from them; it bought collaboration (WebEx 2007, Tandberg 2009) and then security and software (Sourcefire 2013, AppDynamics 2017, Splunk for $28 billion in 2023-24). It joined the Dow in June 2009 and paid its first dividend in March 2011 (6 cents a quarter, raised almost every year to 42 cents by 2026), while repeated restructurings cut thousands of jobs (2011, 2013, 2014, 2024). Chuck Robbins succeeded Chambers in July 2015. For two decades the share price moved mostly between 15 and 60 and the dividend did much of the work for shareholders.

The latest chapter is AI. Demand for data-centre networking pushed the shares past their March 2000 closing high ($80.06) in December 2025, more than 25 years later, although the market value stays below the 2000 peak because of buybacks. In May 2026 record quarterly sales of $15.8 billion and a raised forecast of $9 billion in AI-infrastructure orders sent the shares up 32% in a month, the biggest gain since 2001. For the family that works there, the arc is the classic one: a company that kept growing and never stopped being profitable, but whose stock price at the 2000 peak priced in decades of growth in advance.

## Best and worst moments

- Peak: 2000-03 (+17% in the month, price 77.3), the most valuable company in the world on March 27, 2000.
- Best twelve months: +267% from 1990-08 to 1991-08; the breakout started 1990-10 (+31%) and continued 1990-11 (+34%), 1991-01 (+30%).
- Worst stretch: -86% from 2000-03 to 2002-09. Worst months 2001-02 (-37%, first earnings miss) and 2001-03 (-33%, first layoffs); the inventory write-off came in 2001-05.
- Turning points: 2002-11 (+33%) after the first year-on-year sales growth since the crash; 2011-03 first dividend; 2025-12 back above the 2000 closing high; 2026-05 (+32%) AI orders.
