# Enron: from pipeline company to the most admired stock in America to bankruptcy in fifteen months

Span: 1990-01 to 2001-12 (bankruptcy).

## The story

Enron enters the game in January 1990 as a solid Houston pipeline company: formed in 1985 from Houston
Natural Gas and InterNorth, run by Kenneth Lay, owner of one of the largest gas pipeline networks in the
country. What made it different was an idea from a McKinsey consultant, Jeffrey Skilling: the "Gas Bank",
which turned natural gas into a traded commodity with long-term fixed-price contracts. Skilling joined in
1990, and in January 1992 the SEC accepted mark-to-market accounting for the trading book, which let Enron
book the estimated lifetime value of a contract the day it was signed. Through the early and mid 1990s the
stock did roughly what the market did; international projects (Teesside in England, Dabhol in India) and the
1997 purchase of Portland General made Enron bigger, and some deals (the J-Block gas contract, Dabhol's
political troubles) showed that the long-term bets could go wrong.

From 1999 the story changed. Skilling, president since December 1996, turned Enron into a trading house:
EnronOnline (November 1999) put Enron on the other side of almost every energy trade in North America, and
in January 2000 the company told analysts it would trade internet bandwidth through a network it said was
run by "software intelligence". The stock rose 53% that month and peaked near $90 in August 2000, at about
70 times earnings. Fortune named Enron America's most innovative company six years running.

The warning signs were public, for those who looked. In November 2000 the shares fell a fifth on rumours of
a profit warning that the company denied. In March 2001 Fortune asked "Is Enron Overpriced?", pointing to
rising debt and cash flow that did not match reported profits; the broadband deal with Blockbuster died the
same week. In April Skilling insulted an analyst who asked for a balance sheet. In August he resigned after
six months as chief executive, for "personal reasons", having sold shares worth about $33 million. Each step
took the price down, and each time the company said everything was fine.

The end came in eight weeks. On 16 October 2001 Enron reported a $618 million loss and cut its equity by
$1.2 billion because of partnerships run by its own finance chief, Andrew Fastow; the SEC opened an inquiry,
Fastow was ousted, the banks stopped rolling its commercial paper. In November Enron restated four years of
profits, agreed to sell itself to Dynegy for about $8 billion, and on 28 November lost both the deal and its
investment-grade rating. It filed for bankruptcy on 2 December, the largest in U.S. history at the time.
Employees who had kept their retirement savings in Enron stock lost them; the auditor, Arthur Andersen, was
destroyed. The lesson for players: a company that cannot explain how it makes money, whose debt grows
faster than its profits and whose executives sell and leave, is a risk no matter how admired it is.

## Best and worst moments

- 2000-01: +53%, the broadband analyst meeting; the stock becomes a technology darling.
- 2000-08: the peak, about $90 on 23 August (month +15%).
- 2000-11: -21%, profit-warning rumours denied, California fears; the first crack.
- 2000-12: +28%, Skilling named chief executive, relief rally to $83 at the year end.
- 2001-03: -15%, Fortune's "Is Enron Overpriced?" and the end of the Blockbuster deal.
- 2001-08: -23%, Skilling resigns.
- 2001-09: -22%, doubts about the accounts plus the post-11 September market fall.
- 2001-10: -49%, $618 million loss, SEC inquiry, Fastow out.
- 2001-11: -98%, Dynegy deal collapses, junk rating; worst month.
- 2001-12: +131% from 26 to 60 cents after the bankruptcy filing: speculation in a nearly worthless share.
- Worst stretch: -100% from 2000-09 to 2001-11. Best twelve months: +133% from 1999-03 to 2000-03.

## Unexplained moves

- 1994-11 (-17%, market -4%): no Enron-specific event found. Checked Wikipedia (Enron, Enron scandal,
  Dabhol), the Enron press release archive (starts 1996) and the existing 1980s news files. Possibly the
  bond-market sell-off of 1994 hitting utility-like stocks, but nothing confirms it. The price recovered 13%
  in December.
- 1998-09 (+26%, market +6%): the August fall (-20%) is explained by the market rout after Russia's default
  (beat 1998-08), but no reason was found for a rebound four times the market's. The 1998 press release list
  shows no notable announcement between 24 July and 21 October.
