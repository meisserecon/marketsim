# Intel: from memory chips to the brain of every PC, a quarter-century of decline, and a sudden AI-era revival

Span: 1980-03 to today.

## The story

In 1980 Intel was a memory-chip company with a promising sideline. It had invented the commercial microprocessor in 1971 and the DRAM memory chip before that, but memory was a commodity business, and Japanese makers were winning it on price. IBM's choice of the Intel 8088 for its Personal Computer in 1981 made the sideline matter, and in late 1982 IBM even bought 12% of Intel to see it through the recession. The 1983-84 boom gave Intel its first billion-dollar year. Then came the memory glut, and in October 1985 Grove and Moore did what they later called the best decision in the company's history: they left DRAM, closed plants, cut about a third of the staff, and bet everything on the 32-bit 386 processor, which Intel would make alone without licensing it to rival chipmakers.

The bet paid off for fifteen years. Intel lost money in 1986 and earned a record in 1987. The 486 (1989), the "Intel Inside" campaign (from 1991) and the Pentium (1993) made Intel the largest chipmaker in the world and one of its most valuable companies. Even the Pentium division flaw of late 1994, which Intel first played down and then paid 475 million dollars to fix, barely dented the stock. Under Andy Grove (CEO 1987-1998) the market value rose from about 4 to about 197 billion dollars, and the shares rose roughly twentyfold from the Pentium launch to the peak of August 2000.

The peak ended with a single press release. On 21 September 2000 Intel warned that sales in Europe were weak, and the shares lost a fifth overnight and 44% in the month. What followed was the longest decline in the game: AMD's better chips (2003-06), the 2006 job cuts, a European antitrust fine (2009), the financial crisis, and above all the smartphone, which Intel missed entirely. In February 2009 the stock was 83% below its 2000 peak, and it stayed far below it for a generation while Intel diversified expensively (McAfee in 2010) and gave up on phones in 2016.

The deeper problem surfaced in manufacturing. Intel's lead in chip-making, its real moat, slipped in the late 2010s as its 10- and then 7-nanometre processes ran years late, and TSMC overtook it. Pat Gelsinger's IDM 2.0 plan (2021) was to win back the lead and make chips for others as a foundry, at the cost of huge capital spending. The bill came in 2024: a 7 billion dollar foundry loss, the dividend suspended, 15% of jobs cut, Intel removed from the Dow in favour of Nvidia, and Gelsinger pushed out in December.

Then the story turned. In 2025 the US government took a 10% stake, SoftBank and Nvidia invested, and Lip-Bu Tan took over. In 2026 AI agents turned out to need large numbers of ordinary processors, Intel's foundry won Tesla and Google, and talk of Apple as a customer pushed the shares past their 2000 record in May 2026. April 2026 (+114%) was the best month in Intel's 55 years on Nasdaq; July (-35%) and September (+34%) show how speculative the revival is. The shares rose more than fivefold in the twelve months to June 2026.

## Best and worst moments

- 1985-10: exit from memory chips and launch of the 386, the turning point of the company.
- 1987-01: +49% as the chip slump ended and the market boomed; 1987-10: -32% in the crash.
- 1993-03: the Pentium; the start of Intel's golden age.
- 2000-08: the peak of the first era (about 75 dollars); 2000-09: -44% after the European sales warning.
- 2000-08 to 2009-02: worst stretch, -83%.
- 2002-06: -34% after another forecast cut in the bear market.
- 2020-07: -20% when the 7-nanometre delay and talk of outsourcing showed the lost manufacturing lead.
- 2024-04: -31% (foundry losses disclosed); 2024-08: -28% (dividend suspended, 15% of jobs cut).
- 2025-08 and 2025-09: +23% and +38% on the US government stake and Nvidia's investment.
- 2026-04: +114%, the best month in Intel's history; 2026-05: past the 2000 record; 2026-07: -35%; 2026-09: +34%.
- Best twelve months: +523% from 2025-06 to 2026-06.

A search found a contemporary report that an analyst's bullish comments on Intel sent technology shares sharply higher as investors bet on a recovery after the September 2001 lows (semissourian.com, "Stocks rally higher on Intel"), but the article could not be opened to confirm the date and details, and the 2001 annual report gives no month-specific cause.
