# Coca-Cola: from sleepy blue chip to the great compounder of the Goizueta years, a lost decade after 1998, then a steady dividend stock

Span: 1979-12 (game start; data from 1975-01) to today.

## The story

At the start of the game Coca-Cola is a solid but unexciting blue chip: the world's biggest soft drink company, selling syrup to some 1,500 bottlers in more than 135 countries, with side businesses in juices, wine, coffee, plastics and water treatment. Profits grow 12 to 13% a year, Pepsi is gaining in the US, and the shares fell about a fifth in 1979. Roberto Goizueta, a Cuban-born chemist who becomes chief executive in August 1980 and chairman in March 1981, changes the company's purpose to creating shareholder value. He makes two bold bets in 1982, Columbia Pictures and Diet Coke, survives the New Coke fiasco of 1985 (which ends with Coca-Cola Classic outselling Pepsi), and in 1986 invents the structure the company still uses: Coca-Cola owns brands and sells concentrate, while capital-hungry bottling sits in partly owned bottlers (Coca-Cola Enterprises). He sells the side businesses, sells Columbia to Sony in 1989 at a big profit and buys back shares relentlessly.

The market rewards this richly. Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway reveals a large stake in 1989, a 76% year. The shares gain nearly 73% in 1991, mark time in 1992-93 (the 'Marlboro Friday' scare about branded goods), then rise 44% in 1995 and more in 1996-97. By Goizueta's death in October 1997 the company's market value has gone from 4.3 billion dollars in 1981 to about 147 billion, and a 1,000 dollar investment from 1981 has become about 62,000 dollars with dividends. By then investors pay a very high price for very steady growth.

That premium makes the shares fragile. Asia's currency crisis (August 1997) and Russia's default (August 1998) hit a company that earns most of its profit abroad, while Douglas Ivester's short reign brings a contamination scare in Belgium and France (June 1999), a race discrimination suit, blocked acquisitions in Europe and seven quarters of falling earnings. The shares swing wildly (up 33% by July 1998, down 40%, back up, down again) and peak in mid-1998. Ivester is pushed out in December 1999; Douglas Daft cuts 6,000 jobs. In early 2000 the dot-com rotation out of 'old economy' stocks adds to the pain. The slide lasts until November 2004 (-54% from June 1998), when Neville Isdell, called back from retirement, resets the long-term targets lower and raises marketing spending.

From 2005 the shares recover slowly. Coca-Cola moves into non-carbonated drinks (Glaceau 2007, a Monster stake 2014, Costa 2018), falls with the market in the 2008 crisis but recovers faster, buys back its North American bottler in 2010 and refranchises the territories again under Muhtar Kent and James Quincey. It is now a slow-growing, high-margin dividend stock that holds up in crises and passes on inflation (2022). The pandemic (March 2020, -17%) hurt because half of its revenue comes from restaurants, stadiums and cinemas, but sales recovered with the reopenings. In December 2025 Quincey handed over to Henrique Braun (CEO from March 2026); the shares reached a record of nearly 89 dollars in August 2026.

## Best and worst moments

- Best twelve months: June 1985 to June 1986, +81% (New Coke turned into Coca-Cola Classic, bottler restructuring).
- 1989: total return 76%; July 1989 +21% (unexplained, see below), Buffett stake revealed in March.
- 1991-12: +16% with the market after the Fed's full-point rate cut; +73% in 1991.
- 1997-08: -17%, Asian currency crisis.
- Peak: June-July 1998 (record near 89 dollars before splits in mid-July 1998).
- 1998-08: -19%, Russian default; 1998-10: +17% rebound.
- 1999-09: -19%, profit warning; 1999-10: +22% bounce; 1999-12: -13%, Ivester resigns.
- 2000-02: -15%, old-economy sell-off.
- Worst stretch: -54% from June 1998 to November 2004; bottom when Isdell cut the long-term targets (2004-11).
- 2008-10: -17% in the financial crisis; 2020-03: -17% in the pandemic.
- Today: record month-end price of 88.67 dollars in August 2026 under Henrique Braun.

## Unexplained moves

- 1985-11 (+16%, market +7%): no company-specific news found for the month. Checked the 1985 annual report (no month-level stock discussion), the New Coke and Coca-Cola articles on Wikipedia, and an archive search of UPI headlines. Likely candidates, not confirmed: the general market rally after the Plaza Accord weakened the dollar (good for a company earning most of its profit abroad), and Coca-Cola Classic outselling Pepsi by the end of 1985.
- 1989-07 (+21%, market +9%): about half the move is the market. No company-specific cause found; checked the 1989 annual report, the Berkshire letters for 1988 and 1989, a NYT earnings listing for the second quarter of 1989, and web searches (several rate-limited). Coca-Cola had a 76% year; Berkshire kept buying through 1989.
- 1990-05 (+18%, market +9%): about half the move is the market. No company-specific cause found; checked the 1989 and 1990 annual reports (the two-for-one split was approved at the April 1990 meeting, which does not explain a price gain).
