# Wall Street: booms, crashes and crises: from the Volcker squeeze to the AI boom, the S&P 500 rose about seventy-fold

Span: 1979-12 to today (latest month in the data: 2026-09).

## The story

The game opens with the S&P 500 at 107.94 and inflation above 10 percent. Paul Volcker's Federal Reserve was squeezing money to break inflation, and the market spent 1980 to mid-1982 lurching between credit controls, the silver crash of March 1980, a post-election rally in November 1980 and a 20-month bear market under record interest rates (the prime rate peaked at 21.5 percent in December 1980). The turn came in August 1982: the Fed cut rates, Henry Kaufman of Salomon Brothers stopped predicting higher rates, and the Dow jumped 39 points on 17 August in its biggest one-day gain ever. That month began an 18-year bull market. Its 1980s phase was driven by falling interest rates and a takeover boom financed with junk bonds (Drexel Burnham Lambert, Boesky, RJR Nabisco), with a warning in September 1986 and the climax in 1987: up 39% to the August record, then Black Monday on 19 October, when the S&P 500 fell 20.5% in a day. The crash did not stop the economy, the Fed supplied cash, and the index was back at its old peak in July 1989. The takeover boom ended with the failed UAL buyout (Friday the 13th, October 1989) and Drexel's bankruptcy in 1990.

The 1990s began with the Gulf crisis recession (August 1990) and a burst of Fed easing in December 1991. After the bond shock of 1994 came five years of gains near or above 20% (1995-1999), with Greenspan's 'irrational exuberance' warning of December 1996 three years too early. Two panics interrupted it, both foreign in origin and both short: the Asian crisis (27 October 1997) and Russia's default with the near-collapse of the hedge fund LTCM (August-September 1998), ended by three Fed rate cuts. The dot-com bubble peaked in March 2000. The bust that followed took the S&P 500 down three years in a row, worsened by the 11 September attacks and the accounting scandals of 2002 (Enron, WorldCom), to a low in October 2002 about half below the peak.

Cheap money (a 1 percent Fed rate in 2003-04) carried shares to a new record in October 2007, by which time the credit crisis had already started (BNP Paribas, August 2007). The 2008 crisis is the arc's central episode: Bear Stearns in March, record oil in June, then September (Fannie and Freddie, Lehman's bankruptcy, AIG, the money-fund run, the House vote against the bailout), the October crash with the worst week ever, Citigroup's rescue in November, and the final low on 9 March 2009 at 676.53, 57% below the 2007 peak. The Fed's zero rates and bond buying, and bank earnings, started a bull market that lasted eleven years, interrupted by the flash crash and Greece in May 2010, America's downgrade and the euro crisis in summer 2011, China's devaluation in August 2015, the volatility shock of February 2018 and the Christmas fall of December 2018, reversed when the Fed promised patience.

The pandemic crash of February-March 2020 was the fastest ever, 34% in five weeks, and the recovery the fastest: April 2020 was the best month since January 1987 and the index was at a record by August. Cheap money and stimulus produced the speculative excesses of 2021 (GameStop), then inflation and seven Fed rate rises brought the bear market of 2022, with the low in October. From there an AI-led boom produced three double-digit years in a row (2023-2025), shrugging off the 2023 bank runs (SVB, Credit Suisse), the yen shock of August 2024 and the tariff crash of April 2025. In 2026 the war with Iran and fears of AI disruption pushed shares down 5% in March, a ceasefire and strong earnings produced the best month since 2020 in April, and by September 2026 the S&P 500 stood at 7,651.54, a little below its August record, with long-term interest rates at their highest since 2007 and the Fed raising rates again.

The lessons this arc carries for players: big falls come fast and from many directions; the best months tend to sit right next to the worst (October 1987 and January 1987, October 2008 and March-April 2009, March and April 2020); and in every case so far, those who held on were above water within a few years, except after 2000, when it took until 2007.

## Best and worst moments

- Best month: January 1987, +13% (Dow 2,000), closely followed by April 2020, +13% (recovery from the pandemic crash), and August 1982, +12% (the great turn).
- Worst month: October 1987, -22% (Black Monday). Then October 2008, -17% (worst week ever), August 1998, -15% (Russia and LTCM), March 2020, -13% (pandemic).
- Turning points: August 1982 (+12%, start of the 1982-2000 bull market); October 1987 (-22%) and the low in November (-9%); October 1990 low (year -7%); March 2000 peak (+10%); October 2002 low (+9%); October 2007 record; March 2009 low (+9%); March 2020 low (-13%) and the April rebound (+13%); October 2022 low (+8%).
- Worst stretches: November 1980 to August 1982 (S&P 500 -27%); August to November 1987 (-32%); March 2000 to October 2002 (S&P 500 about -50%); October 2007 to March 2009 (-57%); February to March 2020 (-34%); January to October 2022 (-25%).
- Best calendar years: 1995 (+34%), 1997 (+31%), 2013 (+30%), 2019 (+29%). Worst: 2008 (-38%), 2002 (-23%), 2022 (-19%), 2001 (-13%), 2000 (-10%).
- Flat year as a lesson: 2011 ended at 1,257.60 against 1,257.64, after a 21% fall and recovery.

## Unexplained moves

None. Every S&P 500 month of +-8% or more (rounded, 50 months from 1980-03 to 2026-04) has a beat with a `move`. A few explanations are thinner than the rest, because the only contemporary source that could be read was the Federal Reserve's own record of market developments, and no press account was available:

- 1984-08 (+11%): explained by the sharp fall in bond yields recorded by the FOMC (record of the August 1984 meeting); no press account of the rally was found.
- 1986-09 (-9%): explained by rising Treasury yields and the dollar (FOMC record of September 1986); the famous one-day fall of 11 September 1986 is not cited because no source for it could be read.
- 1987-01 (+13%): the Fed's record only says that stock prices "soared to new highs"; no specific trigger was found beyond the Dow passing 2,000.
- 1989-07 and 1990-05 (+9% each): explained by falling interest rates (FOMC records), which is what the Fed recorded; no press accounts.

Note on the calendar-year figures: the "Calendar years" table in movers.md was mislabelled by one year when this arc was written; all year-end beats use December-to-December figures computed from data/reference/sp500.json.
