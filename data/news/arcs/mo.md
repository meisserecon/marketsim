# Philip Morris / Altria: Marlboro's cash machine survives the litigation years, sheds food and the world, and ends as a US-only dividend stock

Span: 1979-12 (game start; data from 1975-01) to today. Name Philip Morris until 2002-12, Altria from 2003-01. The price series folds in the spin-offs of Kraft Foods (March 2007) and Philip Morris International (March 2008): earlier prices are scaled down so that a holder sees no jump at the spin-offs.

## The story

In 1979 Philip Morris is Marlboro: the best-selling US cigarette since 1976, a market share that rose from 9% in 1961 to more than a quarter, and pricing power shared with R.J. Reynolds. It owns Miller beer (bought 1970, now near the top) and Seven-Up (bought 1978, a flop). Cigarette profits are enormous and growing, and the company uses them to buy food: General Foods in 1985 (5.75 billion dollars, the year Philip Morris passes Reynolds as the largest US cigarette maker) and Kraft in 1988 (13.1 billion, the second-largest takeover in US history). The share price rises about sevenfold from 1985 to 1991, interrupted only by the 1987 crash (-22%). A jury award against a cigarette maker in the Cipollone case (1988), later overturned, is a warning shot.

On 'Marlboro Friday', 2 April 1993, Philip Morris cuts the price of Marlboro by 20% to fight discount brands and the shares fall 26% in a day. Then the litigation years begin: tobacco chiefs deny under oath that nicotine is addictive (April 1994), Liggett breaks ranks and settles (March 1996), states sue for Medicaid costs, a 368.5 billion dollar national settlement fails in Congress (1997), and in November 1998 the industry signs the Master Settlement with the states for at least 206 billion dollars over 25 years. The settlement does not stop individual and class suits. In 1999 the Florida Engle class action threatens punitive damages above 300 billion dollars; when an appeals court orders a lump-sum award in October 1999 the shares fall 23% and talk of bankruptcy begins. From November 1998 to February 2000 the stock loses 64%.

The rescue comes in 2000: Florida caps the appeal bond (May, +19%), Philip Morris buys Nabisco and plans a Kraft listing, and investors fleeing technology buy steady earners. Even a 145 billion dollar Engle verdict (July 2000) barely dents the shares, which gain 90% in 2000, the best of the Dow, and 139% in twelve months. Kraft is listed in 2001 and Miller sold to SAB in 2002. 2002-2003 bring two more scares: a run of verdicts (June 2002, -24%) and a profit warning blamed on state tax increases and black-market cigarettes (September 2002, -22%), then, under the new name Altria, an Illinois 'lights' verdict with a 12 billion dollar appeal bond that the company says it cannot post (March 2003, -22%). In May 2003 the Engle verdict is thrown out on appeal (+34%, the stock's best month), and the existential threat is over.

With litigation contained, Altria breaks itself up: Kraft is spun off in March 2007 and Philip Morris International in March 2008, leaving a purely American tobacco company that buys UST (smokeless tobacco, 2008), comes under FDA regulation (2009), turns its Miller stake into 9.6% of AB InBev (2016) and pays out most of its profit. The shares rise steadily to a peak in 2017. Then Altria bets 12.8 billion dollars on the e-cigarette maker Juul (December 2018), just before the teen-vaping backlash; the stake is written down repeatedly, the FDA orders Juul off the market and announces plans to cut nicotine levels (June 2022, -23%), and Altria swaps the stake for patents and buys NJOY in 2023. Today Altria is a slowly shrinking, high-yield income stock: cigarette volumes fall, prices and dividends rise (60th increase in 56 years in 2025), and the share price remains below its 2017 peak.

## Best and worst moments

- 1987-01: +23% (market +13%) and 1987-08: +20%, both unexplained (see below); 1987-10: -22% in the crash.
- 1991-12: +18% with the market after the Fed's rate cut; the top of the first great run.
- 1993-04: -27%, 'Marlboro Friday'.
- 1999-10: -23%, lump-sum Engle ruling; worst stretch -64% from November 1998 to February 2000.
- 2000-05: +19%, Florida bond cap; best twelve months +139% from February 2000 to February 2001; 2000-10: +24% (unexplained).
- 2002-06: -24%, verdict run; 2002-09: -22%, profit warning.
- 2003-03: -22%, Illinois lights verdict and bond; 2003-05: +34%, Engle verdict overturned (best month).
- Peak: May 2017 (75.44 dollars month-end on the spin-off adjusted series).
- 2022-06: -23%, FDA bans Juul and targets nicotine.

## Unexplained moves

- 1987-01 (+23%, market +13%): more than half of the move is the market rally of January 1987. No company-specific news found; checked the Philip Morris 1987 annual report review (record tobacco income, Japan opening, but no month), the company history, and the archived UPI headline index for January 1987 (Wayback CDX), plus web searches (rate-limited).
- 1987-08 (+20%, market +3%): no cause found. Checked the same sources and the UPI headline index for August 1987; the 1987 report notes strong growth abroad (volume in Japan more than doubled after import duties were suspended), which may have supported the shares, but no August event was found.
- 2000-10 (+24%, market flat): no single cause found. Part of the 2000 recovery (best Dow stock of 2000, +90%): investors expected the Engle verdict to be overturned, a Bush victory was seen as good for tobacco, and money left technology stocks (LA Times, 2 February 2001). Philip Morris filed no 8-K in October 2000; searches for October news were rate-limited.
