# Nokia: from Finnish conglomerate to the world's phone maker, to near-collapse, to networks

Span: 1994-07 to today.

## The story

Nokia enters the game in July 1994, when its American depositary shares trade in New York. It
was a 130-year-old Finnish conglomerate of pulp, rubber, cables, tyres and televisions that
Jorma Ollila, chief executive from 1992, was turning into a pure telecoms company. Its equipment
carried the first GSM call in 1991 and it sold the first GSM phone in 1992. The first crash came
fast: a logistics breakdown and a December 1995 profit warning (-28% in a month). After that
Nokia shed televisions and audio and became the momentum stock of the 1990s, overtaking Motorola
as the world's largest phone maker in October 1998. At the 2000 peak it was 70% of the Helsinki
market and 4% of Finland's GDP.

The telecom bust of 2000-2002 took the ADR down by two thirds in waves (July 2000, February and
June 2001), but Nokia stayed profitable and kept gaining share; a 2004 warning (-31%) proved a
blip. In 2007 Apple unveiled the iPhone; Nokia's market share hit its all-time high of 40% that
quarter and the shares nearly doubled during the year. The decline began in 2008: share warnings,
the financial crisis, a late answer to touch screens. Stephen Elop, hired from Microsoft in
September 2010, bet the company on Windows Phone in February 2011; Symbian sales collapsed before
the Lumia was ready. Warnings in 2011 and 2012 took the ADR below 2 dollars, 96% under the 2000
peak.

In September 2013 Microsoft bought the phone business for 5.44 billion euros (+67%, the best
month in the game). The remaining Nokia was the network venture with Siemens, bought out in
July 2013, plus patents and maps. It bought Alcatel-Lucent (2015-16), struggled in early 5G and
suspended its dividend in 2019 (-28%), and recovered under Pekka Lundmark. From 2025 under Justin
Hotard Nokia was rerated as an AI-infrastructure supplier: Nvidia invested 1 billion dollars in
October 2025 (+44%) and AI and cloud orders drove April 2026 (+61%), before a 31% fall in July
2026 that this arc cannot explain.

## Best and worst moments

- 1995-12: -28%, the first profit warning; 1998-02: +33% (unexplained) and 1998-10, number one.
- 1999-12: +36%, the bubble's last leg; peak in early 2000.
- 2001-02: -36%, 2001-04: +42% (unexplained), 2001-06: -24%, 2001-10: +31%, the bust in waves.
- 2004-04: -31%, market share warning.
- 2000-04 to 2012-06: -96%, the worst stretch; 2012-04: -34% on another warning.
- 2013-09: +67%, the sale of the phone business to Microsoft.
- 2019-10: -28%, dividend suspended for 5G.
- 2025-10: +44% (Nvidia), 2026-04: +61% (AI orders and ceasefire rally), 2026-07: -31%.

## Unexplained moves

- 1995-06 (+29%): checked Wikipedia, the IMD supply-chain case and the Nokia press archive; only
  general strength of demand until late 1995 was found, no specific June news.
- 1998-02 (+33%): the 1997 annual results were probably published around then, but no report of
  them could be read (CNN Money's archive starts with Nokia in October 1998; Nokia's 1998 press
  releases in the Wayback Machine do not include results).
- 2001-04 (+42%): the Nasdaq rallied strongly in April 2001 and the market rose 8%; Nokia's
  first-quarter report (around 19 April) could not be found in the CNN, Nokia or Wayback archives.
- 2026-07 (-31%): Nokia's second-quarter report of 23 July was solid (AI and cloud sales +105%,
  outlook unchanged apart from a technical revision) and the S&P 500 was flat; Intel (-35%) and
  Tesla (-26%) also fell. A sector sell-off after the April-May surge, or the collapse of the
  US-Iran ceasefire on 8 July, are possible, but no source linking either to Nokia was found.
