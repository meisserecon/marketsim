# Oil and energy: from the second oil shock through $10, $147 and minus $37 to the Hormuz crisis

Span: 1979-12 to today.

## The story

The game opens at the top of the second oil shock. The Iranian revolution had cut Iran's exports in 1979, and in December OPEC could not even agree a price: members charged $24 to $30 a barrel, US refiners paid an average of about $23.60, against $13 a year earlier, and spot cargoes went for more. Iraq's invasion of Iran in September 1980 took more oil off the market, and the average price US refiners paid peaked at about $37 in early 1981. Oil companies were the stock market: in 1980 six of the ten largest US companies were oil stocks and about a third of the S&P 500's value was energy. Reagan's election and his decontrol of US crude prices in January 1981 were the last burst of that era.

High prices undid themselves. Rich countries used 13% less oil in 1981 than in 1979, North Sea, Alaskan and Mexican oil flowed, and OPEC's share fell from about half the world market to under a third. OPEC cut its price for the first time in March 1983 (to $29) and set quotas, while crude futures began trading in New York. Saudi Arabia, the swing producer, cut its own output to a third of its 1981 level to hold the price, gave up in 1985, and flooded the market. US crude fell from $26 in January 1986 to $10.25 at the end of March and under $11 in July. OPEC restored a fixed price around $18 in December 1986, and for most of the next thirteen years oil traded between $15 and $20, interrupted only by Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in August 1990, which doubled the price to $41 by October, and the war in January 1991, which collapsed it by $10 in a single day.

The 1990s were the decade of cheap oil. OPEC raised its ceiling just as the Asian crisis hit in late 1997; by December 1998 US crude was $10.82 and Brent $9.10. A joint cut with Mexico, Norway and others in March 1999 began a ten-year climb. After a wobble around the 2001 recession, prices rose with Chinese demand, thin spare capacity, the Iraq war's aftermath, a weak dollar and money flowing into commodities: $56 in October 2004, over $70 after Hurricane Katrina in 2005, $99 in November 2007, and an all-time high of $147.27 on 11 July 2008. Then the financial crisis took the price to $30 by Christmas.

High prices had again brought new supply, this time from US shale. US crude output rose from about 5.5 million barrels a day in 2010 to over 9 million in 2014 while Brent sat above $100 on the Arab Spring. In November 2014 Saudi Arabia refused to cut, aiming to squeeze high-cost shale; with Iran's sanctions lifted and the Paris climate deal adopted, US crude fell to $26 in February 2016. OPEC then allied with Russia (OPEC+, November 2016) to manage supply. Shale recovered and kept growing; the 2019 drone attack on Abqaiq, which briefly took out 5% of world supply, barely moved prices for long. In March 2020 the pandemic and a Saudi-Russian price war collided, and on 20 April the expiring US crude contract settled at minus $37 because there was nowhere left to store oil.

The rebound brought the energy transition into open conflict with supply. The IEA's 2021 net-zero roadmap said no new oil fields were needed; a year later Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent Brent to $133 and US gasoline to a record $4.93, OPEC+ cut output to defend $80-90, and Europe went through its worst gas crisis. COP28 in 2023 called for moving away from fossil fuels while US output reached records above 13 million barrels a day. In 2025 tariffs pushed oil below $60. Then came the largest supply shock in the market's history: after US-Israeli strikes on Iran on 28 February 2026, Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz, Brent recorded its biggest monthly rise ever in March, and physical cargoes touched $138 in April. A ceasefire on 8 April sparked a stock-market rally; the UAE left OPEC on 1 May; a June memorandum briefly brought Brent back to $70 before renewed attacks, now also in the Red Sea, sent physical Brent back to about $131 in September 2026.

Through it all, the oil companies in the game, Exxon and Schlumberger, mostly track the price with leverage: Schlumberger, as an oilfield-services firm, moves far more than Exxon whenever drilling budgets change.

## Best and worst moments

- Peak of the old era: 1980-11, Reagan's election; Exxon +15%, Schlumberger +20%, S&P 500 +10%.
- Collapse: 1986-03, US crude $10.25; the low in July (Schlumberger -16%).
- Recovery: 1987-01, OPEC's $18 accord holding; Exxon +18%, S&P 500 +13%.
- Shock: 1990-08, Iraq invades Kuwait; S&P 500 -9%.
- Low of the period: 1998-12, US crude $10.82, Brent $9.10; in 1998-08 Schlumberger -28%, S&P 500 -15%.
- Turn: 1999-03, the joint OPEC/non-OPEC cut; Exxon +18% in 1999-04.
- Peak: 2008-07, $147.27; then 2008-10, Schlumberger -34%, S&P 500 -17%.
- Shale bust: 2014-11, OPEC refuses to cut (Schlumberger -13%); bottom 2016-02 at $26.
- Worst: 2020-03, price war and lockdowns, Schlumberger -50%, Exxon -26%, S&P 500 -13%; 2020-04, minus $37, yet Exxon +22%, Schlumberger +25%, S&P 500 +13%.
- Reopening: 2020-11, vaccine news, Schlumberger +39%, Exxon +17%, S&P 500 +11%.
- War spike: 2022-03, Brent $133; 2022-10, OPEC+ cut, Schlumberger +45%, Exxon +27%.
- Hormuz: 2026-03, record monthly rise; 2026-04, ceasefire, S&P 500 +10%.

Market months of ±8% that this theme explains, wholly or in part: 1980-11 (+10%), 1987-01 (+13%, in part), 1990-08 (-9%), 1998-08 (-15%, in part), 2008-10 (-17%, in part: oil fell with the market), 2020-03 (-13%, in part), 2020-04 (+13%, in part), 2020-11 (+11%, in part), 2026-04 (+10%).

Calendar years: oil's collapse is part of the background to the 1986 rally (+18%), the 1990 Gulf crisis sits in that year's figures, and the 2008 crash and 2022 spike are part of those years' stories, but no calendar-year move is mainly an oil story.

Moves of the oil companies tied to the oil price (beats with a `move`): Exxon 1980-11, 1987-01, 1999-04, 2005-02, 2020-02, 2020-04, 2021-02; Schlumberger 1986-07, 1990-07, 1998-08, 2000-12 (natural gas), 2006-01, 2008-10, 2010-05 (Deepwater Horizon moratorium), 2014-11, 2018-12, 2020-03, 2020-11, 2022-01, 2022-10, 2025-04, 2026-01. Exxon's 2020-03, 2020-11, 2022-01 and 2022-10 and Schlumberger's 2020-04 and 2021-02 moves are caused by the same beats; each beat records one move only.

## Unexplained moves

- S&P 500 1980-03 (-10%): not an oil month; the average price US refiners paid rose only from $26.11 to $26.88. Left to the market arcs.
- S&P 500 2022-07 (+9%) and 2022-09 (-9%): oil fell in both months (US crude -6% and -11%), so oil does not explain either direction. Falling gasoline prices may have helped the July relief, but no source was found tying the two.
- Schlumberger 1987-10 (-25%): the stock-market crash, not oil (US crude was steady near $20).
- Exxon and Schlumberger 2026-01 (+18%, +26%): oil rose 13% that month amid the capture of Venezuela's president and Iran's crackdown on protests, and the beat records it, but which cause moved the shares most is not settled.
- Schlumberger 2026-08 (+21%) and 2026-09 (-19%): oil was flat in August and up 10% in September; not explained by the oil price.
