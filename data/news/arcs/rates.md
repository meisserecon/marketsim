# Inflation, interest rates, bonds and gold: from 21 percent to zero and back to 5

Span: 1979-12 to today.

## The story

The game opens at the high tide of the Great Inflation. US consumer prices are rising 13 percent a year, the second oil shock is
still working through, and gold is in a speculative frenzy that peaks on 21 January 1980 above 840 dollars (a monthly average of
675 that was not seen again until 2006). Paul Volcker, Fed chairman since August 1979, has switched the Fed to controlling the
money supply and lets interest rates go wherever that takes them. The result is two years of whiplash: credit controls and a
prime rate of 20 percent in spring 1980, a collapse in rates with the short recession, then the real squeeze, with the prime rate
at a record 21.5 percent in December 1980, the rate banks charge each other near 20 percent in mid-1981, and 10-year Treasury
yields at a record 15.3 percent in September 1981. Gold falls by half; anyone holding cash earns 7 to 10 points above inflation.

The turn comes in summer 1982. Inflation drops toward 5 percent, Mexico's default frightens the Fed, and rates are cut in quick
steps. Henry Kaufman's forecast of falling rates on 17 August 1982 sets off the stock market's biggest rally in a generation, and
October 1982 confirms it. That is the beginning of the great bond bull market: long yields fall, with interruptions, from 15
percent in 1981 to 1.5 percent in 2016. The interruptions are the dangerous months for a bond holder in the game: 1984 (yields
back to 13.5 percent), 1987 (a rise from 7 to over 10 percent that set up Black Monday), 1994 (Greenspan's pre-emptive hikes, the
bond massacre), 2003 (a sharp sell-off after the Fed's cut to 1 percent) and 2013 (the taper tantrum). Each time the trend
resumed. Under Greenspan the Fed becomes the market's backstop: the liquidity promise after the 1987 crash, the full-point cut of
December 1991, the three cuts of autumn 1998 after Russia's default and LTCM. His "irrational exuberance" speech of December
1996 is the most famous warning that markets ignored.

Gold has its own arc. After the 1980 spike it spends twenty years in a bear market, with brief rallies when rates fall (1982,
1986-87) and lows near 300 dollars in 1985 and around 250 in 1999, when Britain's announced sale ("Brown's bottom") marks the
capitulation. The Washington Agreement of September 1999, which capped European central-bank sales, is the floor. In the 2000s
gold rises with negative real interest rates, a weak dollar and the financial crisis: back to its January 1980 average in 2006, to records
in 2008, and to a peak monthly average of 1,772 dollars in September 2011 after America lost its AAA rating. Then it falls by
almost 40 percent to the end of 2015, the month the Fed first raised rates after seven years at zero.

After 2008 interest rates are at zero and the Fed buys bonds in three rounds of quantitative easing. Long yields reach record lows
in 2012, 2016 and 2020. The Fed's attempts to normalise (the 2013 taper, the 2015-2018 hikes) unsettle markets each time; the
December 2018 sell-off forces the "patient" pivot of January 2019. The pandemic brings the rate back to zero and unlimited bond
buying in March 2020, and gold to a new record in August 2020 as real yields go deeply negative.

Then inflation returns. Called "transitory" through 2021, it reaches 9.1 percent in June 2022; the Fed answers with the fastest
increases in forty years, from zero to 5.25-5.5 percent by July 2023, including four three-quarter-point steps. Shares and bonds
fall together in 2022 (the 2040 zero-coupon Treasury loses about 30 percent in the year), mortgage rates pass 7 percent, the
British gilt market breaks in September 2022 and Silicon Valley Bank fails in March 2023 on its bond losses. Ten-year yields peak
near 5 percent in October 2023; the Fed cuts from September 2024. Meanwhile gold enters a new bull market driven by central-bank
buying (over 1,000 tonnes a year from 2022 to 2024), the 2025 tariff shock and doubts about the Fed's independence: 3,000
dollars in spring 2025, 4,000 in October 2025, a blow-off above 5,000 in January 2026 and a crash the day Kevin Warsh was named
Fed chairman. In 2026 a war with Iran lifts oil and inflation again; under Warsh the Fed raises rates in September 2026 and
10-year yields are back near 5 percent, where the game stands today.

## Best and worst moments

- Gold's peak: 1980-01, gold +48% for the month (record fixing above 840 dollars on 21 January). Worst stretch for gold: 1980 to
  1999, from a 675-dollar monthly average to 256 in July 1999.
- The squeeze: 1980-03 (credit controls, S&P 500 -10%, gold -17%), 1980-12 (prime 21.5%, gold -14%), 1981-09 (10-year yield 15.3%,
  the low point for bonds).
- The turn: 1982-08 (S&P 500 +12%) and 1982-10 (+11%); a 20-year zero-coupon Treasury gained 83% in calendar 1982.
- Crashes with a rates story: 1987-10 (-22%, after a year of rising yields), 1998-08 (-15%, Russia and the flight to Treasuries),
  2018-12 (-9%, after the fourth hike of the year), 2022-09 (-9%, third three-quarter-point hike and the gilt crisis).
- Rate-cut rallies: 1991-12 (+11%), 1998-10 (+8%), 2001-04 (+8%), 2019-01 (+8%), 2020-04 (+13%).
- Gold's floor and turn: 1999-07 (20-year low of 252.80 dollars) and 1999-10 (+17% after the Washington Agreement).
- Gold's 2000s peak: 2011-08 (+12%), monthly average top in 2011-09; then 2013-04 (-7%) starts the slide to the 2015-12 low.
- Worst year for bonds in the game: 2022, with the 2040 Treasury down about 30% (the S&P 500 fell 19% from December to December).
- Gold's blow-off: 2026-01 (+10% on the month after passing 5,000 dollars, despite a one-day crash of around 10% on 30 January).

Calendar-year turns this theme explains (December to December, computed from data/reference/sp500.json; note that the
"Calendar years" list in movers.md is labelled one year late and runs January to January, see below): 1981 (-10%, the
Volcker squeeze), 1982 (+15%, the turn), 1994 (-2%, the bond massacre), 1995 (+34%, the bond rally after the last hike), 2018
(-6%, Fed tightening), 2019 (+29%, the pivot and three cuts), 2022 (-19%, the inflation shock and the fastest hikes in forty
years), 2023 (+24%, the end of hikes).

## Unexplained moves

- 1986-09, S&P 500 -9% (movers.md rounding; -8.5% unrounded): long Treasury yields rose in the month (20-year zero yield 7.9% to
  8.5%, data/out/ust2000.json) and gold jumped 11%, so a rates and inflation scare is plausible, but no contemporary account of
  the cause was found (checked FRED series, Wikipedia "1986 in the United States", the existing 1986 news items).
- 1990-05, S&P 500 +9%: coincided with a bond rally (20-year zero yield 9.34% to 8.89%, data/out/ust2010.json; the 10-year
  Treasury averaged about 8.8%), but no source was found tying the rally to rates, inflation data or the budget talks of that
  spring (checked FRED, Wikipedia on the early-1990s recession and the 1990 budget act).
- 2022-10, S&P 500 +8%: rates were still rising (the 10-year above 4%, mortgages near 7%, FRED); the rally in the second half of
  the month is often put down to hopes that the Fed would slow its hikes, but no source for that was confirmed here.
- 1984-08 has a beat with a move, but its attribution to the retreat in bond yields rests on the data, not on a contemporary
  account; treat it as probable rather than certain.

Market months of 8% or more that are not in this arc's reach and are left to other arcs: 1980-11 (Reagan's election), 1987-01 and
1987-11 (boom and crash aftermath), 1990-08 (Iraq and oil; this arc has a bond beat), 1997-07, 2000-03, 2000-11, 2001-02,
2001-09, 2001-11, 2002-07, 2002-09, 2002-10, 2003-04 (dot-com, 9/11, Iraq war), 2008-06 to 2009-04 and 2008-10 (financial
crisis; this arc covers the zero-rate and QE response in 2008-12), 2010-05, 2010-09 and 2011-10 (euro crisis and recovery),
2015-10, 2020-02, 2020-03 and 2020-11 (pandemic; this arc covers the Fed's response in 2020-03 and 2020-04), 2026-04 (Iran
ceasefire and earnings). Gold's biggest months in movers.md all have a beat with a move.
