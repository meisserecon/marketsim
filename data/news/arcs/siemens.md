# Siemens: the German conglomerate that took itself apart

Span: 1979-12 to today.

## The story

In 1979 Siemens is Germany's electrical giant: power stations and nuclear plants through its
KWU unit, telephone exchanges for the Bundespost, medical scanners, factory controls, chips,
computers and household appliances. It is cautious and cash-rich; after the 1987 crash its market value was briefly below its cash. Under
Karlheinz Kaske (1981-1992) it spent that cash on breadth: Rolm from IBM and, with GEC, the
British group Plessey (1988-89), then Nixdorf, the failing Paderborn computer maker, in 1990,
which made it Europe's biggest computer company but never a profitable one. The fall of the
Berlin Wall gave the shares their best two months of the decade.

The 1990s under Heinrich von Pierer were flat until the Ten-Point Program of November 1998:
spin off the loss-making chips (Infineon, 1999), sell or close what does not earn its keep. The
internet boom did the rest. Infineon's IPO in March 2000 was 33 times oversubscribed, and Siemens
tripled in fifteen months to a February 2000 peak, then lost 72% by September 2002 as the
telecom equipment business, its biggest exposure to the bubble, collapsed. The handsets went to
BenQ in 2005 (with a dowry), the network gear into Nokia Siemens Networks in 2006.

In November 2006 police raided Siemens over bribery: some 1.4 billion dollars of illegal payments
between 2001 and 2007, mostly through the telecom unit. Von Pierer and his successor Klaus
Kleinfeld left in April 2007; the outsider Peter Löscher settled with US and German authorities in
December 2008 for about 1.4 billion dollars (1.6 billion including an earlier German fine). The
financial crisis and a falling euro produced the worst month in the game (October 2008, -36%).
Löscher exited nuclear power after Fukushima and was ousted in July 2013 after project delays.

Joe Kaeser (2013-2021) completed the break-up: Osram spun off (2013), Vision 2020 (2014),
Mentor Graphics and the Gamesa wind merger (2016), Siemens Healthineers listed (2018), Siemens
Energy spun off (2020). Under Roland Busch the remaining Siemens is a factory-automation,
building-technology, rail and industrial-software company with record profits in fiscal 2022 and
2023, the Altair purchase (2025) and a plan to hand the Healthineers stake to shareholders. Prices
in the game are in dollars, so the mark and euro matter: the euro explains a quarter of the
October 2008 fall and most of the December 2008 rebound.

## Best and worst moments

- 1987-10: -25%, the October crash.
- 1989-11 and 1989-12: +21% and +21%, the fall of the Berlin Wall.
- 2000-02: +31%, the peak of the bubble ahead of the Infineon IPO.
- 2000-02 to 2002-09: -72%, the telecom bust (2001-09 -25%, 2002-09 -29%).
- 2001-10: +27% and 2002-10: +41%, rebounds off market lows; 2002-10 is the best month since 1979.
- 2008-10: -36%, the financial crisis and a 10% fall of the euro; 2008-12 +23% on the bribery
  settlement and a euro rebound; 2009-01 -23%.
- 2022-11: +24% and 2023-11: +27%, record years of the slimmed-down Siemens.
- 2026-03: -19% on the Iran war energy shock; 2026-04: +25% on the ceasefire rally.

## Unexplained moves

None of the "Biggest months" in movers.md is left unexplained. Several are attributed to the
market rather than to Siemens news (1987-10, 2001-09, 2002-09, 2002-10, 2008-10, 2009-01,
2026-03, 2026-04); the 2000-02 jump is linked to the Infineon offer by timing, not by a quoted
cause. Not explained among the smaller moves: 1985-10 and 1985-12 (+22%, +21%; the post-Plaza
German rally and the weak dollar are likely, no source read) and 1988-02/1988-06 (+17% each).
