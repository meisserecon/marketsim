# Schlumberger: the oil boom's most admired stock, which turns every oil cycle into a boom and bust twice as big

Span: 1979-12 to today.

## The story

Schlumberger sells the oil companies knowledge of what lies underground: it lowers measuring instruments into
wells (wireline logging), and around that core it added drilling,
pumping, seismic surveys and equipment. Under Jean Riboud, chairman for two decades, revenue grew by more than a
quarter a year from its 1962 New York listing, and at the 1980 oil peak it ranked fourth by market value behind
AT&T, IBM and Exxon (about 25 billion dollars), priced at 25 times earnings. That peak came in November 1980 (+20% in the
month). The company then did everything a player should learn to fear: it had bought a chipmaker (Fairchild, 425
million dollars in 1979) and in 1984 bought the offshore driller Sedco for about 1 billion, near the top of the
rig market. Profits peaked in 1982 at a record 1.35 billion dollars, more than any other of the world's 1,000 largest
companies, while the shares had already halved: the market looks ahead.

The 1986 oil collapse was the first catastrophe. Oil fell from 24 dollars to 7, the number of active US rigs
halved, and Schlumberger reported a loss of 2 billion dollars for 1986, its first since listing, after writing off
1.74 billion of Sedco Forex and Dowell goodwill and rigs. Riboud had died in October 1985; his successor was
ousted in October 1986, and Euan Baird, the first non-Frenchman to run the company, refocused it on oilfield
services. A Fujitsu takeover of Fairchild was blocked by Washington, and Fairchild went to National Semiconductor
for 122 million in August 1987. Two months later the crash took a quarter off the shares (-25% in October 1987).

Under Baird the company rebuilt slowly (profit doubled in 1988, Dowell fully bought in 1993), and the deepwater
and international boom of 1995-97 pushed profit up 52 percent in 1997 to 1.3 billion; the shares passed their 1980
high in 1996 and more than tripled from 1994 to 1997. Then the 1998 oil crash: crude at 13 dollars and the August
1998 market panic cost 28 percent in a month. In 1999, at the bottom of the rig market, Schlumberger got rid of its
drilling business: Sedco Forex was spun off to shareholders on 30 December 1999 and merged into Transocean. The
proceeds of that clarity were wasted on the second diversification mistake: Sema, an IT services company bought
for 5.2 billion dollars in February 2001 at the top of the tech bubble, was written down by 2.9 billion in
December 2002 and sold to Atos for 1.5 billion in 2003, when Andrew Gould took over.

Gould's Schlumberger was a pure oilfield company in the right place for the 2004-08 boom: record results, a split
and a 19 percent dividend increase sent the shares up 31 percent in January 2006; they peaked in mid-2008 and lost
a third in October 2008 alone. The 2010s were the era of mega-deals, Smith International (11.3 billion dollars,
2010) and Cameron (14.8 billion, 2015, bought in the bust), and of the shale boom and its glut. From June 2014 to
March 2020 the shares lost 89 percent, the worst stretch in the game: oil halved in 2014-15, 2019 brought an 11
billion dollar loss on write-downs under the new chief executive Olivier Le Peuch, and the pandemic and price war
of March 2020 halved the stock in one month (-50%), below its 1980 peak forty years later. In April 2020 it cut
the dividend by 75 percent.

The recovery was just as violent: +25% in April 2020, +39% on the vaccine news in November 2020, +26% in February
2021, +30% in January 2022 when Le Peuch spoke of a new "supercycle", and +45% in October 2022, its best month,
when international and offshore activity returned and the company renamed itself SLB (the game keeps the name
Schlumberger). The supercycle faded in 2023-25 (in 2025 revenue fell in Saudi Arabia, Mexico and Africa), and the
ChampionX purchase (2024-25) shifted the company toward production from existing wells. In 2026 SLB rose 26 percent in January on an upbeat
outlook and dearer oil, then found its most important region, the Middle East, disrupted by the Iran war. Over the
whole game Schlumberger is the lesson in leverage to a cycle: the same oil price moves that shifted Exxon by 20
percent moved Schlumberger by 40 to 50.

## Best and worst moments

- Peak of the first act: 1980-11, +20% (Reagan rally; fourth by market value behind AT&T, IBM and Exxon).
- First loss in its history: reported 1987-02 (2 billion dollars for 1986).
- Crash: 1987-10, -25%.
- Late-1990s peak: 1997-10; then 1998-08, -28% (oil at 13 dollars, market panic).
- Spin-off: 1999-12, Sedco Forex to Transocean.
- Biggest blunder of the second act: 2001-02, Sema purchase (-17%), written off 2002-12.
- 2000s high point: 2006-01, +31%; peak mid-2008; 2008-10, -34%.
- Worst stretch: -89% from 2014-06 to 2020-03; worst month 2020-03, -50%.
- Dividend cut by 75%: 2020-04 (+25% that month anyway).
- Best twelve months: +116% from 2020-10 to 2021-10; best month 2022-10, +45%.
- 2026-01, +26% (outlook, oil, Venezuela), then Middle East disruption from 2026-03.

## Unexplained moves

- 2000-12 (+29%, market +0%): no company or industry event found. The month followed a 19 percent fall in
  November 2000, which coincided with the collapse of technology shares. Checked: Schlumberger's EDGAR filings
  (no 8-K between May 2000 and February 2001), the Wikipedia articles on Schlumberger and WesternGeco, FRED oil and
  gas prices. US crude fell in December (monthly average 34 to 28 dollars), so oil is not the cause; US natural
  gas, however, reached a record (Henry Hub monthly average 5.52 dollars in November, 8.90 in December), which
  would favour North American drilling, but no source was found linking it to the share move.
- 2026-01 (+26%) is covered by a beat, but the size is only partly explained: the upbeat 23 January outlook and a
  13 percent rise in the oil price are documented, the role of Venezuela (where SLB has long operated) is not.
