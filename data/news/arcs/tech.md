# The technology waves: four booms, two busts, and the same names lifted and dropped together

Span: 1979-12 to today.

## The story

Four waves of technology run through the game, and each one lifts the technology companies together before the stock market decides which of them deserve it. The first is the **personal computer**. Apple goes public in December 1980; IBM's PC of August 1981, built from Intel's chip and Microsoft's software, turns a hobby into office equipment. The excitement peaks in June 1983, when the Nasdaq closes at a record 328.91, and breaks within months: Texas Instruments and Commodore drive home computers down to 100 dollars, Osborne goes bankrupt and the Nasdaq loses a third by July 1984. In 1985 memory chips collapse from 3.50 dollars to 35 cents under Japanese competition, and Intel leaves the memory business for microprocessors, the decision that made it. The Macintosh (1984) shows the future of using a computer, but Windows 3.0 (May 1990) and Windows 95 (August 1995) bring it to the IBM-standard PC, and the profits of the PC decade go to Intel and Microsoft rather than to IBM, Apple or Compaq.

The second wave is the **internet**. The World Wide Web opens in 1991, the Mosaic browser in 1993, Netscape in 1994, and Netscape's IPO on 9 August 1995 (28 dollars, closing at 58.25, worth 2.9 billion dollars without profits) starts the mania. Greenspan's "irrational exuberance" in December 1996 comes more than three years early. From late 1998 first-day gains of several hundred percent become normal (theglobe.com, +606%), the Nasdaq gains 85.6% in 1999 and peaks at 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000, when Cisco briefly becomes the world's most valuable company. Then it falls: 25% in one week of April 2000, 23% in November 2000, 22% in February 2001 as the telecom bubble (more than 500 billion dollars of mostly borrowed money for fibre and mobile licences) bursts, until the bottom of 1,114 on 9 October 2002, 78% below the peak. Pets.com, WorldCom and most of the 1999 class of IPOs disappear; Amazon, eBay, Priceline and Nvidia survive and multiply. The Nasdaq needs until April 2015 to close above its 2000 record.

The third wave is **mobile, cloud and social**. Google's IPO (August 2004) proves that the web makes money; Amazon starts renting computing power in 2006; the iPhone (announced January 2007), the App Store (July 2008) and Android (September 2008) turn the phone into the main computer. Nokia, with 40% of the world phone market in 2007, gives up its own software for Microsoft's in 2011 and sells its phone business in 2013, while Apple becomes the world's most valuable company in 2011. Facebook's IPO (May 2012, 104 billion dollars) disappoints at first. The cloud business, first disclosed by Amazon in April 2015, becomes the profit engine of Amazon and Microsoft. The pandemic of 2020 pulls years of digital life forward; the Nasdaq goes from 6,861 in March 2020 to 16,057 in November 2021, then loses a third in 2022 as interest rates rise.

The fourth wave is **artificial intelligence**. AlphaGo's win in 2016 is an early sign; ChatGPT (30 November 2022) the starting gun. Microsoft invests heavily in OpenAI; Nvidia's forecast of 24 May 2023 puts the boom into profit figures, and Nvidia becomes the most valuable company in the world (June 2024), worth 4 trillion dollars in July 2025 and 5 trillion in October 2025. The scares come and go: DeepSeek wipes about 600 billion dollars off Nvidia in one day in January 2025. From autumn 2025 the AI money reaches the old names of earlier waves, Intel and Nokia above all, and in April 2026 the Nasdaq has its best month since 2020 and semiconductors their best since February 2000. The July 2026 crash of the Korean memory-chip makers is the first serious break. Where it stands at the end of the game is open; the parallels with 1999-2000 are drawn by many, and the curator can let players draw them too.

The lesson across all four waves: the technology is real every time, and the early market leaders are often not the long-run winners (IBM, Apple in 1985, Netscape, Nokia). Prices run years ahead of profits, and the busts take everyone down, the survivors included.

## Best and worst moments

- 1981-08: the IBM PC; the first wave starts.
- 1983-06: Nasdaq record 328.91; then the home-computer shakeout (Apple -38% in 1983-09 on its own troubles).
- 1985-12: the chip bust; Intel leaves memory chips.
- 1987-01: computer shares lead a January surge (Microsoft +52%, Intel +49%, Apple +37%; market +13%).
- 1987-10: the crash hits tech hardest (Nasdaq -27%, Intel -32%, Apple -32%; market -22%).
- 1990-05: Windows 3.0 (Microsoft +26%, Intel +22%; market +9%).
- 1995-08: Netscape IPO and Windows 95.
- 1999-12: Nasdaq +22% in the month, +85.6% in 1999 (Sony +55%, JDS Uniphase +45%, Nokia +36%).
- 2000-03: the peak, Nasdaq 5,048.62 on the 10th (market +10%).
- 2000-04: the week of -25% (Microsoft -34%, Pets.com -33%).
- 2000-11: Nasdaq -23%, its worst month since 1987 (market -8%; Priceline -57%, Pets.com -44%, JDS Uniphase -39%).
- 2001-01: the Fed's surprise cut, Nasdaq +14% in a day (Priceline +102%, Nvidia +58%, Apple +45%, Microsoft +41%).
- 2001-02: the telecom bust (market -9%; JDS Uniphase -51%, Amazon -41%, Cisco -37%).
- 2002-10: the bottom at 1,114 on the 9th, -78% (market +9%; Siemens +41%, IBM +35%).
- 2015-04: the Nasdaq back above its 2000 record.
- 2022-11: ChatGPT.
- 2023-05: Nvidia's forecast; the AI boom begins in earnest.
- 2025-01: the DeepSeek shock.
- 2026-04: the chip rally (market +10%; Intel +114%, Nokia +61%, Viavi +57%).
- 2026-07: the Korean chip crash (Intel -35%, Nokia -31%).

## Unexplained moves

- 1987-01 (market +13%, Nasdaq +12%): the beat gives the figures and the 386 recovery as context, but no source was found for what drove computer shares in that particular month; the broad rise belongs to the 1987 boom in the market arc.
- 2000-03 (market +10%): the Nasdaq itself fell 2.6% in March 2000. The beat attributes the S&P's gain to a last surge of big technology names (Cisco the most valuable company, the S&P record on 24 March) and a move out of small dot-coms into large companies; no contemporary month-end report could be read to confirm the rotation, so the `move` should be read as shared with the market arc.
- 2002-09 (market -11%) and 2026-04 (market +10%) are given `move`s here but have other causes too (accounting scandals and recession fears; the US-Iran ceasefire). The market arcs may claim them as well.
- Company months that the waves touch but that the company arcs own (not given a `move` here): Apple 1983-09, Intel 2000-09, Apple 2000-09, Cisco 2001-03, Intel 2025-09, Intel 2026-04, Intel and Nokia 2026-07. The theme beats name the context.
- The movers list's calendar-year table appears to be labelled one year late (for example "2009: -40%" matches the S&P's 2008, "2003: -24%" the S&P's 2002); the year-turn beats here use the actual calendar years.
- 2026-02 (IBM -22%) and 2026-05 (Cisco +32%, IBM +29%): possibly AI-related, but no source could be read; left to the company arcs.
