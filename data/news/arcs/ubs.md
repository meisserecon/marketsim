# Schweizerische Bankgesellschaft / UBS: from the biggest Swiss bank to a merger, a state rescue and the takeover of its last rival

Span: 1982-03 to today.

## The story

The Schweizerische Bankgesellschaft (Union Bank of Switzerland, SBG) entered the 1980s as the largest of the three big Swiss banks, run by Robert Holzach and Nikolaus Senn: conservative at home, a leading Eurobond underwriter abroad, protected by bank secrecy (confirmed by the voters in May 1984) and by a domestic banking cartel. Its share was a bet on the Zurich market and the franc; the 1985 boom gave it its best twelve months (+112% from April 1985), the 1987 crash took a third off. Its first big venture abroad, the London broker Phillips & Drew, bought in 1986, lost £48 million in the crash and cost £115 million to prop up, a pattern that would recur.

The 1990s were a decade of humiliation. The Swiss property crash (the run on the Spar- und Leihkasse Thun in October 1991 marks its start) left the Bankgesellschaft with bad loans to developers, culminating in a 3-billion-franc charge and its first loss since the Second World War for 1996. In January 1993 the Kreditanstalt snatched the Volksbank from under its nose. From 1991 the financier Martin Ebner built up a large stake through BK Vision and fought management over the plan to unify the share classes. In April 1996 the board rejected Rainer Gut's offer to merge with CS Holding. In January 1997 a night guard, Christoph Meili, rescued Nazi-era files from the bank's shredder, turning the dormant-accounts dispute into Switzerland's worst foreign-policy crisis since the war; it ended with the $1.25 billion settlement of August 1998, paid by UBS and Credit Suisse.

On 8 December 1997 the Bankgesellschaft and the Bankverein announced their merger; it took effect at the end of June 1998 under the name UBS, with the Bankgesellschaft as the legal shell and the Bankverein's Marcel Ospel as chief executive. Weeks later Russia defaulted and LTCM collapsed. UBS, LTCM's biggest bank partner through an option deal, lost about 950 million francs; the share fell 25% in August and 40% in September, chairman Cabiallavetta resigned, and the share bounced 40% in October. The 2000s were the Ospel years: PaineWebber (2000) made UBS a giant in American wealth management, the single UBS brand (2003) and the investment bank grew, and the bank's refusal to extend credit to Swissair in October 2001 brought demonstrators onto the streets against Ospel.

The US mortgage crisis exposed the strategy. From the closure of the Dillon Read hedge fund in May 2007 to the write-downs of December 2007 (capital from Singapore), April 2008 (Ospel out, a 15-billion-franc rights issue) and the Lehman month, UBS lost about 86% of its value from April 2007 to March 2009. On 16 October 2008 the National Bank took up to $60 billion of toxic assets off its hands and the Confederation put in 6 billion francs. In February 2009 UBS paid $780 million to the US and handed over client names, the start of the end of bank secrecy; Oswald Grübel came in as chief executive. The recovery was swift (+49% in April 2009, +26% in August when the US tax case was settled and the state converted its notes and sold at a profit), but the 2011 rogue-trader loss of $2.3 billion cost Grübel his job. His successor Sergio Ermotti turned UBS into a wealth manager first in October 2012 (+23%), the decision that defines the second half of the arc.

That decision is why UBS, and not Credit Suisse, was standing in March 2023, when the Swiss authorities forced it to take over its rival for 3 billion francs under emergency law. UBS gave back the state guarantees in August 2023 and booked about $29 billion of negative goodwill; Ermotti returned to run the integration. The open question at the time of writing (October 2026) is how much extra capital Bern will make the last Swiss big bank hold: the Federal Council's June 2025 proposal implied about $24 billion more. The share is up about 9.5-fold in dollars since 1982 (+847%), but most of that came before 2007; in September 2026 it was still about a quarter below its April 2007 peak in dollars.

## Best and worst moments

- 1985-10 (+18%): the 1985 boom; best twelve months +112% from 1985-04 to 1986-04.
- 1987-10 (-17%): the crash.
- 1997-12 (+14%): merger with the Bankverein announced.
- 1998-08 (-25%), 1998-09 (-40%), 1998-10 (+40%): Russia, LTCM and the rebound.
- 2007-04 peak, then the worst stretch: -86% to 2009-03, with 2008-05 (-28%, rights issue), 2008-09 (-25%, Lehman), 2008-11 (-25%) and 2009-02 (-25%, US tax settlement and record loss).
- 2008-10 (+1%): the state rescue.
- 2009-04 (+49%) and 2009-08 (+26%): the rebound and the US-Swiss tax agreement.
- 2010-07 (+27%): back to profit.
- 2011-09 (-20%): rogue trader and the franc cap.
- 2012-10 (+23%): Ermotti's retreat from fixed income.
- 2020-11 (+22%): vaccine rally.
- 2023-03 (-3%), 2023-08 (+20%): Credit Suisse takeover and the bargain becoming visible.

## Unexplained moves

None of the twelve biggest months is unexplained; each has a beat with a move. Weaker links: 2008-11 (-25%) is explained by the general bank sell-off and the US tax case reaching management (Raoul Weil stepping aside), without a single trigger; 2010-07 (+27%) by the second-quarter profit and a firmer franc. Not explained (not in the biggest-months list): 2025-12 (+21%), 2026-02 (-12%), 2026-04 (+15%) and 2026-09 (-11%); no sources were read for 2025-2026 beyond UBS's 2025 annual report.
