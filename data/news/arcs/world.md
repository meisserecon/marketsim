# The world: politics and power: from the Cold War's last decade to tariffs and war in Europe

Span: 1979-12 to today.

## The story

The game opens in the coldest winter of the late Cold War. Soviet troops entered Kabul at Christmas 1979, Carter answered with a grain embargo, a doctrine for the Gulf and an Olympic boycott, and gold went to 843 dollars in January 1980. Ronald Reagan's landslide that November gave Wall Street one of its best months of the decade, and the early 1980s were a decade of confrontation: martial law in Poland, the new American missiles in Europe in 1983 and the peace camps against them. The turn came with Mikhail Gorbachev in March 1985. The summit in Geneva that November, the INF treaty of 1987 and the Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan led to 1989: Solidarity's election victory in June, the opening of the Berlin Wall on 9 November and the fall of the regimes from Prague to Bucharest. Germany was reunited in October 1990, and after the failed coup of August 1991 the Soviet Union dissolved itself at Christmas 1991.

For a family in Switzerland the 1990s were about Europe. The Maastricht treaty of December 1991 set the course for the euro, survived the Danish no, the French "petit oui" and the currency crisis of September 1992, and the euro was born in January 1999, with notes and coins in January 2002. Switzerland chose its own path: on 6 December 1992 it rejected the European Economic Area by 50.3 percent, and instead built a set of bilateral treaties, approved by 67 percent in 2000, followed by Schengen in 2005. The image of the Swiss banks suffered in the dormant-accounts affair, settled for 1.25 billion dollars in 1998, and bank secrecy for foreign clients cracked when UBS handed client data to the United States in 2009. Three times the state stepped in for a national icon: Swissair, grounded in October 2001 when the banks would not keep it flying; UBS, rescued by the Confederation and the National Bank in October 2008; and Credit Suisse, pushed into UBS over a weekend in March 2023.

The United States set the pace of world politics after 1991: Clinton's prosperous 1990s, the disputed election of 2000, the attacks of 11 September 2001 (the market's worst week in decades and the start of two wars), the Iraq invasion of 2003, Obama's election in the middle of the financial crisis, and Donald Trump twice. Each Trump victory moved the market: banks in 2016, Tesla in 2024. In his second term his tariffs of April 2025 shook markets worldwide and hit Switzerland hardest in Europe in August 2025, with 39 percent, before a deal cut the rate to 15 percent in November; the Supreme Court struck down the legal basis of most of these tariffs in February 2026.

Europe's other story is crisis management. The euro crisis began with the Greek bailout of May 2010, came back in the summer of 2011 together with the downgrade of the United States, and ended only when Mario Draghi promised in July 2012 to do "whatever it takes". Britain voted to leave the EU in June 2016 and left in January 2020. Switzerland's own relationship with the EU stayed unresolved: voters backed limits on immigration in 2014, and the government broke off the framework talks in 2021.

Russia's path runs through the arc as a slow return to war: the default of 1998 that brought Putin to power at the end of 1999, the war with Georgia in 2008, the annexation of Crimea in 2014, and the full invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, which also pushed neutral Switzerland to adopt the EU sanctions. The pandemic of 2020 was the arc's biggest market event: the virus reached Europe in February, the world closed in March (S&P -13%), and the money of governments and the news of a vaccine and of Biden's victory in November brought the strongest recovery of the period. The Iran war of 2026 and its ceasefire, which explain March and April 2026, belong to the Middle East arc.

## Best and worst moments

- 1980-01: gold +48% on Afghanistan, Iran and inflation; the peak is days away.
- 1980-11: Reagan's victory, S&P +10%.
- 1989-11 and 1989-12: the Wall opens; German shares the strongest in the game (Siemens +21%, then Daimler-Benz +34%), not tied to a cited report.
- 1990-08: Iraq invades Kuwait, S&P -9%, the worst month between 1987 and 1998.
- 1992-12: the Swiss EEA no, the turning point of Swiss European policy (no price move in the game).
- 1998-08: Russia defaults, S&P -15%; Credit Suisse -30%.
- 2001-09: 9/11, S&P -8%, Boeing -35%.
- 2010-05: the Greek bailout, S&P -8%.
- 2011-10: the euro summit's Greek haircut, S&P +11%.
- 2016-06: Brexit, Credit Suisse -22%, UBS -17%.
- 2016-11: Trump's first win, Citigroup +15%.
- 2020-02 to 2020-04: the pandemic, S&P -8%, -13%, then +13%.
- 2020-11: Biden and the vaccine, S&P +11%.
- 2022-02: Russia invades Ukraine; Glencore +15% while the market fell.
- 2023-03: the end of Credit Suisse, -71%.
- 2024-11: Trump's second win, Tesla +38%.

## Unexplained moves

Market months of 8% or more that this arc does not explain, because their cause lies outside politics and power:

- 1980-03 (-10%): Carter's credit controls of 14 March are a political act, but the move belongs to the interest-rate story (Volcker, Silver Thursday); checked the 1980 news items.
- 1982-08, 1982-10, 1984-08, 1987-01, 1987-10, 1990-05, 1991-12, 2000-03, 2001-02, 2002-09, 2008-10, 2009-02, 2009-04, 2022-07, 2022-09: rates, the crash of 1987, the dotcom boom and bust and the financial crisis. In December 1991 the Soviet Union ended, but the +11% followed the Fed's rate cut of 20 December; in September 2022 the British mini-budget crisis was political, but the S&P fell on the Fed.
- 2018-12 (-9%): a US government shutdown began on 22 December and the US-China tariff war ran all year, but the month is usually put down to the Fed and growth fears; left to the Asia and rates arcs.
- 2026-04 (+10%) and 2026-03 (-5%): the US-Israeli war on Iran from 28 February 2026, the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the two-week ceasefire agreed in April; covered by the Middle East arc.

Note for maintainers: the calendar-year figures in movers.md look shifted (its "2009: -40%" matches the S&P 500's fall from late 2007 to late 2008, and "2003: -24%" the fall of 2002), so the year turns were not used here.
