# Exxon / ExxonMobil: the steady giant that lives with the oil price, from 1980 oil king to Dow outcast to 2026 record

Span: 1979-12 to today.

## The story

Exxon starts the game as the largest oil company in the world and America's largest industrial company, the
former Standard Oil of New Jersey. The oil shocks of the 1970s had filled its coffers, and it spent part of the
money on things it did not understand: electric motors (Reliance Electric, bought for 1.24 billion dollars in
1979), office machines (Exxon Office Systems) and shale oil in Colorado. The shares peaked with the oil boom
after Reagan's election in November 1980 (+15% that month) and drifted down until mid-1982 as the oil price
fell. What set Exxon apart in the 1980s was discipline: from mid-1983 it bought back its own shares (a tenth of
them by 1985), it cancelled the Colony shale project in May 1982, sold Reliance and its New York tower in 1986,
and cut 6,200 jobs when oil collapsed that spring. Unlike the oilfield-service companies, Exxon kept earning
money through the 1986 collapse, and its shares more than doubled from the end of 1984 to the summer of 1987.

The Exxon Valdez spill of March 1989 is the most famous event in its history and the least visible in its share
price. The clean-up (an 880 million dollar charge in 1989, about 2 billion in total), the 1.025 billion dollar
settlement with the governments in 1991 and the 5 billion dollar punitive verdict of 1994 were all small against
Exxon's earning power; the verdict was cut by appeals to about 500 million by the Supreme Court in June 2008.
The 1990s were the quiet compounding years under Lee Raymond (chairman from 1993): rising profits, buybacks, a
share split in 1997. At the bottom of the oil market, with crude below 11 dollars, Exxon agreed in December 1998
to buy Mobil, reuniting two pieces of Standard Oil. The merger closed on 30 November 1999 and the company is
ExxonMobil from December 1999 in the game.

The 2000s were ExxonMobil's decade. Oil rose from about 25 to 145 dollars, and profits followed: 17.7 billion
dollars in 2000, 25.3 billion in 2004, 36.1 billion in 2005 (with Rex Tillerson taking over from Raymond in
January 2006) and 45.2 billion in 2008. February 2005 (+23%) was the month the market accepted the oil boom.
ExxonMobil held up well in the 2008 crash thanks to its balance sheet, then made two big bets on US shale: XTO
Energy (41 billion dollars, December 2009), just before gas prices collapsed, and much later Pioneer (2023).

From 2014 the oil glut began the worst stretch of its time in the game: -68% from April 2014 to October 2020.
Profits halved in 2015, reserves were written down in 2017, Tillerson left to become Secretary of State and
Darren Woods took over in 2017. The pandemic and the Saudi-Russian price war of March 2020 (-26% that month,
after -17% in February) pushed it to its low point; it cut spending by 30 percent, kept its dividend, and in
August 2020 was dropped from the Dow Jones average after 92 years. Then the cycle turned as violently as it had
fallen: the vaccine news of November 2020 (+17%), oil above 60 dollars in February 2021 (+21%), the 2022 energy
boom (+24% in January, +27% in October on a 19.7 billion dollar quarter) and a record 55.7 billion dollar profit
for 2022, with an activist fund having won board seats along the way. The windfall paid for Pioneer.

In 2026 the stock ran to an all-time high: oil rose in January with the Venezuela intervention and unrest in Iran
(+18%), and the Iran war from 28 February, with the Strait of Hormuz closed, sent oil above 100 dollars. In July
2026 ExxonMobil moved its legal home from New Jersey to Texas. Over the whole game the stock is the archetype of
a cyclical blue chip: it never came close to failing and its quarterly dividend in data/out/xom.json never goes
down (apart from a split artefact in 2001-06), but a player who bought at the 2014 high waited until October 2022
for the price to be back.

## Best and worst moments

- Best month: 2022-10, +27% (record third-quarter earnings of 19.7 billion dollars; 2022 energy boom).
- Best twelve months: +98% from 2020-10 to 2021-10 (vaccine rally and oil recovery).
- Peak of the first act: 1980-11, +15% (Reagan rally, top of the oil boom).
- Best month of the 1980s: 1987-01, +18%; of the 1990s: 1999-04, +18% (OPEC cuts after the 1998 low).
- Turning point: 1998-12, Mobil merger agreed at the bottom of the oil market.
- 2000s high point: 2005-02, +23%; 2006-01, record 36.1 billion dollar profit and Tillerson.
- Worst stretch: -68% from 2014-04 to 2020-10.
- Worst month: 2020-03, -26% (price war and pandemic), after 2020-02, -17%.
- Symbolic low: 2020-08, dropped from the Dow; share price bottom 2020-10.
- All-time high: 2026-03 (+11% that month, Iran war).
- Disaster with little price effect: 1989-03, Exxon Valdez (0% that month).

## Unexplained moves

None of the twelve "Biggest months" is left without a beat. Partly explained:

- 2026-01 (+18%): the oil price rose about 13% during the month (Venezuela intervention, unrest in Iran) and
  ExxonMobil reported strong 2025 results on 30 January, but no single company event explains a move this
  large; the clash between Woods and Trump over Venezuela (9-11 January) would, if anything, have weighed on the
  shares. Checked: Wikipedia articles on the Venezuela intervention and the price of oil, ExxonMobil's 8-K
  filings of January 2026, FRED daily WTI.
- 1980-11 (+15%): the UPI reports confirm that oil and defence shares led the post-election rally, but no
  Exxon-specific news was found.
