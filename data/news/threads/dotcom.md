# The New Economy: the dotcom bubble, 1994 to 2002

A free web browser from a sixteen-month-old start-up, Netscape, showed in August 1995 that investors would pay billions for internet growth without profits. Greenspan's question about "irrational exuberance" in December 1996 came more than three years too early; Amazon listed in 1997 with losses and a famous brand, and by late 1998 first-day jumps of several hundred percent (theglobe.com) had turned the boom into a gold rush. In 1999 nearly ten million Americans traded online, many of them as day traders, VA Linux rose 698% on its first day, a bare domain name fetched 7.5 million dollars, and the Nasdaq rose 86%. AOL bought Time Warner with its own inflated shares in January 2000, seventeen dotcoms advertised at the Super Bowl, and in March 2000 the Nasdaq passed 5,000 and Cisco, whose routers carried the internet, became the most valuable company in the world. From April 2000 the bust came in waves: a 25% fall in one week, the closure of Pets.com and a 23% Nasdaq month in November 2000, then the telecom and equipment crash that brought Cisco its first layoffs in March 2001, and finally a low in October 2002, 78% below the record. The thread stops there; WorldCom's fraud and Enron belong to their own arcs.
