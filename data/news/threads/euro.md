# The euro and its crisis, 1991 to 2015

At Maastricht in December 1991 Europe agreed on a single currency with entry tests for inflation, deficits and debt. When the first eleven members were chosen in May 1998 the tests were already bent: Belgium and Italy came in with debts twice the limit, while Greece was kept out, only to be admitted in June 2000 and to join in January 2001 on the strength of a reported 1999 deficit of 1.8%. Euro cash arrived in 2002. In November 2004 Eurostat found that the Greek deficit had in fact been above 3% in every year it looked at, including 1999; nothing much happened. In October 2009 the new Papandreou government revealed a deficit of 12.7%, and the euro crisis began: Greece lost market access and took a 110 billion euro rescue in May 2010, Ireland followed in November 2010 and Portugal in May 2011, and markets lumped the weak countries together as the "PIIGS". In autumn 2011 banks were made to write off half their Greek bonds, Italy's borrowing costs passed 7% and the prime ministers of Italy and Greece fell; in June 2012 Spain needed up to 100 billion euros for its banks while about half its young people were out of work. Mario Draghi's promise in July 2012 to do "whatever it takes" ended the panic on the markets, though not the unemployment. The thread closes with the Greek referendum of July 2015, a resounding No to the lenders followed within a week by a third rescue on harsher terms.
